Module 1 · 3 min read

What Is FOB?

The simple version

FOB stands for Functional Oscillation Ballast.

An elastic band is attached to a weight. You start the weight moving. It pulls back toward you. You catch that force, send it out again, and keep the cycle going.

That is it. You begin the movement, and then you stay in a conversation with it.

Why it exists

Most resistance training is built from separate repetitions. You lift, you lower, you stop, you start again. The stopping is built into the design.

FOB was made to ask a different question: what happens if the return of the resistance becomes part of the work instead of the end of it?

What this means for your training

You are not only producing force. You are also receiving it, and that is a separate skill. Most people can start a movement. Fewer can control what comes back at them, especially when tired.

Where you have felt this already

  • Carrying a sloshing bucket of water without spilling it
  • Holding a dog that pulls on the leash
  • Steadying a toddler who shifts their weight without warning

In all three, something is pushing back and changing. You adjust continuously rather than in separate reps.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking it is just a band workout. The moving weight is what makes it different. A band alone does not have to be caught.
  • Trying to go fast first. Speed makes the return harder to receive. Control comes before speed, always.

Established practice Resistance training requires the body to produce force against opposition. Bands store energy as they stretch and return it as they shorten. Both are basic and well established.

Working hypothesis Whether combining elastic return with a moving weight produces adaptations distinct from other training methods requires study specific to FOB. That study has not been done yet.

The exact names, if you like precision

The elastic part is the FOBand. The protective weighted part is the FOBlock. Together they make the FOBallast, which is the tool you hold and move.

The method of using it is called SSORT, short for Self-Sustaining Oscillatory Resistance Training. Training with it is called FOBbing.

Key takeaway

FOB is resistance that comes back to you. Your job is to keep it useful.

Feel it yourself

Start something and catch it

  1. Hold a light object in one hand, arm out in front.
  2. Toss it gently a few inches up and catch it, then again, without pausing between.
  3. Now do ten in a row with no stop at any point.

The catching is the part your body had to learn. That is the half of the movement FOB trains on purpose.

How FOB Resistance Works

The simple version

Different tools create resistance in different ways.

  • A dumbbell resists you because gravity pulls it down.
  • A cable machine redirects a falling weight through a pulley.
  • A band resists you more the further you stretch it.

FOB combines the last two ideas. There is elastic tension that grows as you stretch, and there is a weight with real mass that has to be sped up, slowed down and turned around.

Why it feels different

A dumbbell does not get heavier because you straightened your elbow. A band does get tighter as the system lengthens.

So the resistance is not one fixed number. It changes while you move, depending on how far you have stretched and how fast the weight is travelling.

What this means for your training

You cannot switch off partway through. With a dumbbell you can coast at the easy part of the range. With FOB the hardest moment often arrives when the weight is coming back at you, which is exactly when most people stop paying attention.

A useful comparison

Pushing a child on a swing. You push, the swing leaves, and then it returns whether you are ready or not. Your timing decides whether the next push is smooth or awkward.

That returning energy is free. Using it well is a skill.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the tightest band available. More tension is not automatically better. The right band lets you keep a clean path and enough repetitions to actually train something.
  • Fighting the return instead of receiving it. The energy coming back is useful. Absorb it and redirect it rather than blocking it.

Established practice Elastic resistance generally rises as the band lengthens, though the exact curve depends on the material, its dimensions, its age and the setup. Mass resists changes in motion, so speeding a weight up and reversing it creates demands that band tension alone does not describe.

Key takeaway

The resistance changes while you move. That is the feature, not a flaw.

Feel it yourself

Feel tension change

  1. Take any elastic band. A hair tie will do.
  2. Stretch it a little and hold. Note the effort.
  3. Stretch it twice as far and hold. Note it again.

That difference is happening continuously during a FOB set, in both directions.

Understanding Oscillation

The simple version

Oscillation just means moving back and forth around a point. A pendulum oscillates. A guitar string oscillates. A swing oscillates.

In FOB, the weight oscillates because the band keeps pulling it back while you keep sending it out.

FOB did not invent this

Oscillatory training has existed in sports science for decades and is used by many coaches who have never heard of FOB.

Traditional oscillatory methods usually emphasise very rapid, short-range contractions, aiming to improve how quickly the nervous system can produce force. Some approaches focus on fast alternation between opposing muscles, or on how sharply acceleration itself changes.

That work is real and useful. FOB applies the same underlying principle toward a different goal.

How FOB uses it differently

FOB is not chasing the fastest possible oscillation.

The aim is to build the ability to repeatedly start force, receive force, redirect force, hold a rhythm, and keep the quality of all of that as fatigue builds.

Speed is a variable you can adjust. It is not the target.

What this means for your training

A set is not judged by how fast it looked. It is judged by whether the path, the size of the movement and your position held together from the first repetition to the last.

Common mistakes

  • Treating speed as the score. Going faster while the movement shrinks is not progress.
  • Assuming oscillation is one thing. Frequency, size and path are separate variables. Changing all three at once tells you nothing about what improved.

Established practice Oscillatory training methods exist in the sports science literature and predate FOB by decades.

Observed pattern In FOB sessions, people who hold a steady rhythm tend to keep movement quality longer than people who start fast. This is consistent across sessions but has not been formally measured.

The three variables worth naming

Frequency is how often the cycles happen. Amplitude is how big each one is. Path is the route the weight and your body actually follow.

A useful instruction names which one you are changing. "Go faster" is not useful if the movement collapses to half its size. "Make it bigger" is not useful if control disappears.

Key takeaway

Oscillation is old and well studied. What FOB adds is the demand to keep receiving and redirecting it well while you tire.

Feel it yourself

Find your rhythm

  1. Let one arm hang loose at your side.
  2. Swing it gently forward and back, small and even.
  3. Now keep exactly that size and speed for thirty seconds.

Notice how much attention it takes to keep it the same. That attention is the skill.

The Oscillation Loop

The simple version

Every FOB repetition has four parts:

  • Initiate. You start the motion.
  • Receive. The weight comes back and you take that force.
  • Redirect. You send it along the path you intended.
  • Repeat. You do it again without losing control.

At higher speeds these blur together. Learning them separately makes the sequence much easier to understand.

Why the loop matters

Most beginners can do the first part. Starting a movement is easy.

FOB training really begins at the second part. Controlling the return is where the skill lives, and it is the part conventional training rarely asks for.

What counts as a repetition

A rebound is not automatically a repetition. It becomes one when it is governed.

A repetition counts only while the direction, the range, the rhythm and your body position still meet the standard for that exercise. The moment they do not, you are just moving a weight around.

Why you never fully rest

In a normal set there is a small rest between each repetition. In FOB, receiving and redirecting are still work, so that gap mostly disappears.

Different muscles work harder at different moments, but you stay engaged the whole way through. Movement, muscles, breathing and attention all stay connected until you decide to stop.

Common mistakes

  • Counting rebounds instead of repetitions. If the path fell apart, it did not count.
  • Holding your breath. The set is continuous, so your breathing has to be too.
The Reactive Fatigue Threshold

At some point in a set, fatigue starts to cause a clear loss of speed, movement size or control. That moment has a name: the Reactive Fatigue Threshold.

In many upper-body movements it tends to arrive somewhere around 25 to 40 oscillations, though the exact point changes with the person, the movement and the resistance.

It is useful because it tells you when a set has done its job. You can train to reach it later, to lose less after reaching it, or to recover faster before the next one.

Observed pattern The 25 to 40 oscillation range is a pattern seen in FOB sessions rather than a published finding. Treat it as a starting expectation, not a rule.

Key takeaway

Initiate, receive, redirect, repeat. The second one is the one people skip.

Feel it yourself

Name the four parts out loud

  1. Bounce a ball against the floor with one hand, continuously.
  2. Say the four words as they happen: initiate, receive, redirect, repeat.
  3. Do ten without breaking the rhythm.

You have been doing this loop your whole life. FOB adds resistance to it and asks you to hold it together while tired.

What Your Body Learns

The simple version

Muscles do not have fixed jobs. In one moment a muscle might drive the movement, in the next it might brake the return, and in the next it might hold you steady while something else moves.

Prime mover, brake and stabiliser are roles, not permanent job titles. The same muscle can switch between them within a single repetition.

Control comes before size

When you first improve at a new exercise, most of that improvement is coordination. Cleaner timing. Less wasted tension. Better transfer of force through the body.

Your FOB skill will probably improve before any meaningful muscle growth happens. That is normal, and it is still a real improvement.

What this means for your training

Getting better at FOB does not automatically mean every muscle involved got stronger. It may mean you learned the timing and the path. Both matter, but they are not the same claim.

What your trunk is doing

Your torso sits between your limbs and the ground and helps pass force between them.

During standing FOB work, your arms might create the visible movement while your trunk quietly stops you from being pulled, twisted or folded out of position.

The goal is not to brace as hard as possible. It is to create enough control in the direction the task needs, while you keep breathing and moving.

Common mistakes

  • Bracing everything, all the time. Rigid is not the same as controlled. You still have to breathe.
  • Assuming one target muscle. Describe the job first. The muscles involved change with position and direction.

Established practice Early gains in a new exercise are largely attributed to improved coordination rather than muscle growth. This is standard in the motor learning literature.

Established practice Fatigue is task specific. Someone who copes well with pressing movements may tire quickly during rotational work, because the coordination and local demands differ.

Key takeaway

You will get better at the skill before you get bigger. That is coordination, and it counts.

Feel it yourself

Notice a muscle changing jobs

  1. Hold a light object at arm’s length in front of you.
  2. Move it slowly left, then right, then back to center.
  3. Pay attention to your opposite side while you do it.

The side that was not moving worked the whole time. It was stopping you from rotating.

Reactive Endurance

The simple version

Reactive Endurance is the ability to keep producing and controlling force while the resistance returns, changes direction, and gets harder to manage because you are tiring.

It is the thing FOB is built to develop.

How it differs from strength

Maximal strength measures how much force you can produce in one defined task, usually while fresh.

Reactive Endurance measures how much useful strength and control you still have after repeated work has started to wear you down.

Both are real. They are simply different questions.

What this means for your training

The interesting part of a set is often the later part. If your first five repetitions look excellent and your last five fall apart, that gap is the thing worth training.

Where this shows up in life

  • A mover whose tenth box looks like the first
  • A barber holding their arms up through a full day of clients
  • A parent carrying a child through a city
  • A painter working overhead for hours

Common mistakes

  • Calling every hard set conditioning. A strict raise might end at your shoulders long before your heart is challenged. Those are different limits and worth naming.
  • Judging by how it felt. Someone can feel awful while their output holds steady, and someone else can feel composed while their movement quietly falls apart.

Established practice Neuromuscular fatigue depends on joint angle, contraction type, intensity, duration, recovery, muscle group, motivation and environment. Assessment should separate measured decline from perceived effort.

Working hypothesis Reactive Endurance is a proposed training category. The name and framework come from FOB, and validating it as a distinct measurable quality would require research that does not yet exist.

An honest note about running comparisons

Running repeatedly loads the legs and can improve running economy and tissue tolerance when programmed well.

FOB may create repeated demands for the upper body and torso, but the exact adaptations need FOB-specific study. FOB is not running for the arms, and it does not replace your sport. Runners still need to run. Swimmers still need water.

Key takeaway

Strength asks how much you can do. Reactive Endurance asks how much you keep once you are tired.

Feel it yourself

Find your own drop-off point

  1. Hold your arms straight out to the sides, palms down.
  2. Make small circles, the same size, at the same speed.
  3. Notice the exact moment the circles start shrinking on their own.

That moment is what Reactive Endurance training is aimed at. The goal is to push it later.

Four Ways FOB Builds Work Capacity

The simple version

Most people judge fitness by what someone can do while fresh. Work and daily life also ask what you can do after hours of effort, or on the fifth attempt, or when the task suddenly changes.

FOB organises that into four named abilities.

The four

  • Durability. Holding your quality across a long stretch of work. The later part of the job still looks controlled.
  • Fatigability. How quickly your performance drops while you are working. Lower is better: the drop comes later and is smaller.
  • Repeatability. Resting briefly and performing well again. The first effort is not the only one that matters.
  • Resilience. Staying in control when the task changes. A new direction, position, or speed.

How you train each one

  • Durability: longer work at a speed you can control. Keep the movement looking the same from start to finish.
  • Fatigability: steady work with little interruption. Watch for the first clear drop, and try to delay it over time.
  • Repeatability: several matching rounds with the same rest. Compare each round to the first.
  • Resilience: start with one movement you control, then add one planned change. Keep the original rhythm.

Why they are not the same

A durable person might handle continuous work and still recover poorly between hard efforts. A repeatable person might reproduce short efforts but fade over a long one.

Programming gets better when you name which one you are actually trying to improve, instead of calling all four "not getting tired".

Common mistakes

  • Chasing discomfort. A session worked if quality lasted longer, not if it hurt more.
  • Changing everything at once. If resistance, speed, range and rest all change together, you may improve without learning anything about why.

Established practice Durability, fatigability, repeatability and resilience are terms drawn from performance science. FOB translates them into training targets rather than inventing them.

Key takeaway

Name which of the four you are training. "Getting tired less" is four different problems.

Feel it yourself

Test your repeatability

  1. Do ten quick air squats. Note how the last one felt.
  2. Rest exactly thirty seconds.
  3. Do ten more. Compare the last one to the first set.

The difference between those two sets is repeatability. That is one of the four, measured in about a minute.

Why Direction Matters

The simple version

A vector is just a force with a direction. "Push forward", "pull back", "drive down" and "turn to the side" are all vector instructions.

Changing the direction changes which muscles work, how your trunk has to stabilise, and how hard the movement feels.

Direction is part of the exercise

It is not decoration added afterwards. Two movements that look similar can be completely different jobs if the force arrives from a different angle.

What this means for your training

You can stay standing in one spot and still change the problem entirely, just by changing where the force comes from and where you send it.

This is a large part of why FOB does not need much space.

What antirotation actually means

Antirotation is not a special category of exercise. It describes a job.

Something is trying to twist you, and you produce enough force to stop it. You might look almost still. The absence of visible rotation is the successful outcome, not proof that nothing happened.

Common mistakes

  • Judging by how it looks. A simple movement can be highly relevant. A complicated one can be useless. Complexity measures how much is happening, not whether it is doing anything for you.
  • Thinking stillness means nothing is happening. Some of the hardest work looks like standing still.

Established practice Force direction changes muscle recruitment and stabilisation demand. This is basic biomechanics and is not specific to FOB.

A worked example

A normal Romanian deadlift is useful because of its force relationship, not because it looks impressive. Gravity pulls down, the hips travel back, the posterior chain returns you to standing.

Put more load on one side and it becomes a different problem. You still have to hinge, but now you also have to resist being pulled into rotation or side bending. It did not become "functional" by becoming flashier. The vectors changed.

Key takeaway

Direction is not a detail. It is the exercise.

Feel it yourself

Change one thing and feel it change

  1. Press your palms together in front of your chest and push.
  2. Now move your hands to your left hip and push the same way.
  3. Now up above your head.

Same push, three completely different jobs for your trunk. That is what changing a vector does.

Movement Categories, Lanes & Styles

The simple version

FOB movement can be organized with three different ideas. They answer three different questions.

  • Movement Category: what kind of training demand the movement belongs to.
  • Movement Lane: where you are positioned relative to the anchor.
  • Movement Style: the specific way you choose to perform the movement.

Do not collapse them into one thing. A style belongs to a category, and that style may work in one, two, or all three lanes.

The movement categories

The current working model uses three main categories, with a fourth available when a movement genuinely blends demands.

  • Reactive Core: the torso helps receive, reverse, redirect, or control the returning oscillation.
  • Push / Pull: repeated upper-body muscular action is the main local challenge. This can include curls, lateral raises, pushdowns, presses, rows, and similar patterns even when they are not textbook push-or-pull exercises.
  • Isometric: the athlete maintains a chosen position while the coach manually creates the oscillatory disturbance.
  • Hybrid, when needed: a style can meaningfully combine categories, such as a tennis-swing-like movement that blends Reactive Core with Push / Pull.

The three movement lanes

The current movement lanes are Front, Left, and Right. A lane describes the athlete's orientation to the anchor, not the movement itself.

Not every style works in every lane. A useful movement library records the lanes where each style can actually be performed well instead of forcing symmetry where it does not exist.

What makes a Movement Style a style

A Movement Style is the specific strategy used inside a category and lane. The important details include which part of the oscillation you choose to explode on, how you receive or redirect the return, and how you rotate, position, and coordinate your arms, limbs, and torso.

That means two people can use the same category and lane while performing different styles. Styles can be created or adjusted around the user's fitness level, coordination, strength, experience, and training goal, as long as the movement remains controlled and appropriate for the setup.

The movement library is still being built

This classification is an operating framework, not a claim that every possible FOB movement has already been discovered. New styles are tested, described, assigned to a category, and checked across Front, Left, and Right before they are placed into a session.

A simple field record can stay small: Movement Style · How It Is Performed / Specific Notes · Movement Category · Possible Lanes.

Key takeaway

Category tells you what kind of demand it is. Lane tells you where it can happen. Style tells you exactly how you perform it.

Training Progression

The simple version

Progression means making the training a bit harder over time, in a way you can actually track.

FOB has many things you could change: resistance, position, direction, speed, size of movement, how long you work, how many repetitions, how long you rest.

Change one thing at a time

Because those variables interact, a good progression names what changed.

If resistance, speed, range and rest all change at once, you might get better while learning nothing about why. A strong progression changes one main thing and holds the others steady.

How to measure a set

Counting repetitions makes your output visible, but it can reward rushed, shrinking cycles.

Timing a set is simpler to coach, but it can hide a decline.

The practical answer is both: set a time, count the repetitions that actually met the standard, and note whether your technique held.

What progress looks like

  • You work longer without your posture or rhythm changing
  • You complete more clean repetitions at the same resistance
  • Your later rounds stay closer to your first round
  • You handle a change of direction without losing the rhythm

Common mistakes

  • Adding resistance first. It is the most obvious variable and often the wrong one. Range, duration and control usually have more room to grow.
  • Counting repetitions that did not count. If the path collapsed, the number is fiction.

Established practice Progressive overload requires isolating variables to interpret results. This is standard programming practice across all resistance training.

Key takeaway

Change one thing, write down what it was, and compare honestly.

Feel it yourself

Set your own quality floor

  1. Pick any repeated movement you can do for a minute.
  2. Before you start, decide out loud what "good" looks like.
  3. Stop the moment it stops meeting that, even if you could continue.

That decision, made before fatigue arrives, is most of what good programming is.

Starting Safely

The simple version

Oscillatory resistance adds moving parts and returning force. That is the whole point, and it is also the reason safety is about predictability.

Before you move anything faster, you should already know how to stop it.

The rule that matters most

If the only way to finish a set is an uncontrolled catch, the exercise has already progressed too far.

Stopping is part of the exercise, not what happens after it.

The teaching order

Learn it in this sequence, one cue at a time:

  • Set up. Check the equipment, the connections and your space.
  • Start. Begin the motion deliberately.
  • Receive. Take the returning force.
  • Redirect. Send it back along the intended path.
  • Continue. Hold the rhythm.
  • Stop. End under control, before position is lost.

Your last repetition should still meet the standard of the exercise.

Discomfort versus warning

Muscular effort and burning are normal.

These are not things to push through:

  • Sharp pain
  • Joint pain
  • Numbness, tingling or other neurological symptoms
  • Dizziness
  • Chest pain
  • Unusual shortness of breath

If any of those appear, stop, and speak to a qualified professional before continuing.

How to scale down

If something is too much, you have options before quitting: lower resistance, shorter sets, smaller movements, simpler directions, supported positions, or longer recovery.

Established practice Clear stopping criteria and load appropriate to current ability are standard safety practice in resistance training.

Key takeaway

If you cannot stop it calmly, it is too much. That is the whole rule.

Feel it yourself

Practice stopping first

  1. Before your first FOB set, start the movement small.
  2. Stop it completely, on purpose, at a moment you choose.
  3. Do that three times before you add any speed.

Deciding when to stop, while you are still fresh, is what keeps the end of a set safe.

Common Questions

Do I need to be fit already?

No. Resistance, speed and range all scale. A beginner and an experienced athlete can do the same movement at very different settings.

Is this cardio or strength?

It can lean either way depending on how it is programmed. A strict, slow set may end at one muscle group long before your heart is challenged. A longer continuous set feels quite different.

Naming which one you are after makes the session better.

Will it build muscle?

It is resistance training, so it creates the kind of demand that can contribute to that. But your first improvements will probably be coordination rather than size, and FOB-specific research on hypertrophy does not exist yet.

Does it replace my sport?

No. Runners still need to run. Swimmers still need water. FOB may develop qualities that help, but specific training stays specific.

Is it safe for my back or shoulders?

That depends entirely on you, and it is not a question a website can answer. If you have a current injury or a condition you are managing, speak to a qualified professional first.

FOB does not claim to prevent or treat injury.

How much space do I need?

Enough to complete the full movement and stop it safely, with nothing breakable in range. Much of FOB can be done standing in one spot.

How long until I am good at it?

Controlling the return is a skill, and skills improve with repetition. Most people feel a clear difference in their timing within a few sessions.

Key takeaway

If a question here mattered to you, it is worth asking in a session. Written answers cannot see how you move.

Mindful Eating For Physique Goals

Think in repeatable portions, not perfect meals

Meal Grade judges one visible meal. Your physique is shaped more by the pattern you can repeat across the week. A simple way to keep portions consistent without weighing every bite is to use your own hand as a portable reference.

The hand guide

  • Palm — a protein-dense portion
  • Fist — a non-starchy vegetable portion
  • Cupped hand — a carbohydrate-dense portion
  • Thumb — a fat-dense portion

Precision Nutrition uses roughly one to two palms of protein and fists of vegetables at each meal, with one to two cupped-hand carb portions and thumb-sized fat portions at most meals. Its classic guide starts many men around two portions of each category and many women around one, then adjusts up or down for body size, activity, appetite, and goals.

Calibrate instead of guessing

Start with a consistent plate, eat slowly enough to notice when you are comfortably satisfied, and change one main portion at a time. If your goal or training demand changes, the carb and fat portions are easy places to scale while keeping protein and produce visible.

These are starting references, not a medical prescription. Your calorie goal, training, hunger, performance, and body-weight trend still tell you whether the portions need to move.

Source: Precision Nutrition hand-portion guide

Key takeaway

Use your hand to make portions repeatable, then let your real progress decide whether those portions should move up or down.

What it changes. Speed, size and path are separate things you can change. Name which one you are changing.

Everyday example. A pendulum, a guitar string, a swing.

Related. Frequency, Amplitude, Path

Oscillation loop

The four-part cycle: initiate, receive, redirect, repeat.

Why it matters. It is the structure of every FOB repetition.

What it changes. Most people can initiate. The training really begins at receive.

Everyday example. Bouncing a ball continuously with one hand.

Related. Reactive Fatigue Threshold, SSORT

Reactive Fatigue Threshold

The point in a set where fatigue starts causing a clear loss of speed, movement size or control.

Why it matters. It tells you when a set has done its job.

What it changes. You can train to reach it later, to lose less after it, or to recover faster before the next set.

Everyday example. The moment your arm circles start shrinking without you deciding to shrink them.

Related. Reactive Endurance, Fatigability

Vector

A force together with the direction it travels.

Why it matters. Direction changes which muscles work and how hard something feels, so it is part of the exercise.

What it changes. You can change the whole problem without moving your feet, just by changing the direction.

Everyday example. "Push forward" and "pull down" are both vector instructions.

Related. Antirotation, Path

Antirotation

Producing enough force to stop something from twisting you.

Why it matters. It describes a job, not a category of exercise.

What it changes. You may look almost still. The absence of visible rotation is the success, not proof nothing happened.

Everyday example. Holding a heavy bag on one side without letting it turn you.

Related. Vector, Trunk

Amplitude

How big each oscillation is.

Why it matters. Speed means nothing if the movement is quietly collapsing.

What it changes. "Go faster" is not useful if amplitude falls apart. Decide which one you are protecting.

Everyday example. A big arm swing versus a small one.

Related. Frequency, Path, Oscillation

Frequency

How often the cycles happen.

Why it matters. It is one of the three things you can change, and changing it changes the demand.

What it changes. Higher frequency makes timing errors harder to correct.

Everyday example. Fast small swings versus slow big ones.

Related. Amplitude, Path

Path

The route the ballast and your body actually follow.

Why it matters. A repetition only counts while the path holds.

What it changes. If the path drifts, the repetition stopped counting even if you kept moving.

Everyday example. The line a pendulum traces.

Related. Amplitude, Frequency, Vector

Durability

Keeping your movement quality across a long stretch of work.

Why it matters. It is one of the four work-capacity outcomes.

What it changes. Train it with longer work at a speed you can control. The end should look like the beginning.

Everyday example. A painter working overhead all afternoon.

Related. Fatigability, Repeatability, Resilience

Fatigability

How quickly your performance drops while you are working.

Why it matters. Lower fatigability means the drop comes later and is smaller.

What it changes. Watch for the first clear change in speed, range or control. That is your marker.

Everyday example. A worker whose first ten packages move fast and next ten do not.

Related. Reactive Fatigue Threshold, Durability

Repeatability

Resting briefly and being able to perform well again.

Why it matters. The first effort is rarely the only one that matters.

What it changes. Do matching rounds with the same rest and compare each to the first.

Everyday example. A delivery worker climbing several flights across a shift.

Related. Durability, Resilience

Resilience

Staying in control when the task changes.

Why it matters. Real work rarely happens in one perfect line.

What it changes. Add one planned change at a time and keep the original rhythm.

Everyday example. A mechanic reaching from an awkward new angle.

Related. Repeatability, Vector

Quality floor

The standard you decide on before a set, below which a repetition no longer counts.

Why it matters. It turns "how many" into "how many that were real".

What it changes. Decide it before fatigue arrives, because fatigue is a poor judge.

Everyday example. Saying "the moment my back rounds, I stop" and meaning it.

Related. Path, Reactive Fatigue Threshold
